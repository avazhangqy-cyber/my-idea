# Value-Aligned AI Reflective Diaries - Oral Defense Script

## Slide 1 - Title

Hi everyone, my name is Ava Zhang.

My project is called "Value-Aligned AI Reflective Diaries for Adolescent Emotional Support."

The main idea is not that AI should replace therapists, and it is not that AI should diagnose minors. My question is more careful than that. I wanted to know whether an AI system, if it is guided by clear values and safety rules, can generate low-risk reflective content that helps students organize their feelings and identify one safer next step.

So I define this project as an anonymous, non-diagnostic, privacy-first support tool.

## Slide 2 - Research Question

My research question is: Can a value-aligned AI reflective diary help students feel more understood and identify a safer next step?

I think this matters because many high school students do not ask for formal help right away. When they face academic pressure, uncertainty about the future, family conflict, relationship stress, or identity-related concerns, they may not be ready to talk to a parent, teacher, counselor, or friend.

But they might be willing to write one anonymous concern first.

At the same time, this is a risky space. An AI tool for minors should not act like a therapist. It should not diagnose, label identity, predict who someone is, collect school names or contact information, or encourage users to depend on AI.

So the real question is not "Can AI do therapy?" My question is: Can AI provide bounded, supportive reflection before formal help-seeking?

## Slide 3 - Method

My study used a simple pre-post design.

First, users opened the website and saw a consent and privacy screen. The page made clear that this tool was not counseling, not diagnosis, and not crisis intervention. It also told users not to enter names, schools, contact information, or exact locations.

Second, users completed a five-item pre-survey. The items asked whether they felt understood, less alone, more accepting of their confusion, clearer about one safer next step, and more patient toward their concern.

Third, users selected or entered a concern. Then the website generated an AI reflective diary. The diary was not supposed to give a diagnosis or advice like a therapist. It was designed to turn the concern into an anonymous story, offer a nonjudgmental reframe, and suggest one small safer action.

Finally, users completed the same five-item survey again. I compared the pre-survey and post-survey scores to see whether the tool was associated with any change.

## Slide 4 - Sample and Cleaning

The data came from my Supabase database.

Using the formal released dataset, meaning records submitted on or after July 11, there were 44 participant submission records. But not all of them could be used for the main analysis. Some records were incomplete, and some did not include the full AI diary experience.

After cleaning, the core valid sample used 24 complete records. These are the records where the user completed the pre-survey, the post-survey, and generated an AI reflective diary.

Ten of these records also included complete open-ended feedback. I treat that feedback as usability evidence, not as the main statistical result.

## Slide 5 - Findings

The main finding is that scores increased in the complete pre-post sample.

For the 24 core complete records, the average total score increased from 18.46 to 20.50 out of 25. That is an average increase of 2.04 points.

In terms of direction, 14 users improved, 8 stayed the same, and 2 decreased.

I also checked the 10 complete feedback records. Nine users selected that the anonymous diary made them feel understood, and one selected that the diary was average. This is encouraging, but the feedback subset is small.

So I would describe the result cautiously. The data suggests that the tool may provide early emotional support for some students. But this does not prove clinical effectiveness, and it does not prove that AI caused the change.

## Slide 6 - Meaning

The main implication is that the commercial value of this product should not be "AI replaces therapy."

A safer and more realistic value proposition is: AI lowers the first barrier to help-seeking.

This tool could work as a front-door support layer. A student who is not ready to talk to someone directly can first use an anonymous space to organize a concern, feel less judged, and identify one small next step. If the situation is serious, the tool should point the student toward a trusted adult, a school counselor, a support line, or emergency help.

From a business perspective, I think this is more appropriate as a B2B product than a direct-to-minors product. Possible customers could be schools, youth programs, counseling centers, or mental health education platforms.

But safety cannot be an extra feature. It has to be the center of the product. The tool should not diagnose, treat, imitate a human therapist, collect sensitive identity data, or encourage emotional dependence.

## 30-Second Short Closing

If I need to close quickly, I would say:

Overall, this project does not argue that AI can provide therapy for minors. It argues for a more limited and safer possibility: with clear values and safety boundaries, AI may help students reflect anonymously, feel more understood, and identify one safer next step. In my formal released dataset, the 24-record core sample increased by an average of 2.04 points. But because the sample is small, nonrandom, and has no control group, I treat this as early supportive evidence, not proof of effectiveness. Thank you.
